The answer depends on how closely you measure it.

So how long is Great Britain's coastline?

There isn't a single simple answer.

For instance, if you isolate strictly the mainland island of Great Britain (excluding the Hebrides, Orkney, Shetland, Anglesey, the Isle of Wight, etc.), the official Ordnance Survey length is approximately 11,073 km (6,881 miles).

This measurement uses the Mean High Water (MHW) mark — the average level reached by the sea at high tide — which includes tracing the tidal boundaries of rivers and estuaries as far inland as the tide reaches.

Why does this number vary so much from simpler measurements? Because the smaller the measuring interval, the more of the coastline's tiny irregularities we capture — and the longer the measured coastline becomes.

This is known as the coastline paradox.

The coastline isn't physically getting longer. We're simply measuring more of its complexity.

So where should we stop?

Should we measure every kilometre? Every metre? Every centimetre?

At increasingly small scales, we begin measuring rocks, pebbles and even grains of sand.

The answer depends on the scale at which you choose to measure the coastline.
