In 1967, mathematician Benoit Mandelbrot analyzed this exact phenomenon in his famous paper, "How Long Is the Coast of Britain?"

He realized coastlines exhibit self-similarity: repeating patterns of bays within bays and headlands within headlands, no matter how closely you zoom in.

So how long is Great Britain's coastline?

There isn't a single, definitive answer.

For instance, the official Ordnance Survey length for mainland Great Britain is approximately 11,073 km (6,881 miles). But that figure relies on standardizing the boundary at the Mean High Water (MHW) mark.

In reality, the concept of a single coastline "line" is itself a human construct. Defining a boundary between sea and land requires picking a tidal datum — a reference level between high and low tide that is never flat relative to Earth's surface, and is in constant, dynamic flux.

Layer on top of that Mandelbrot's fractal geometry: coastlines don't behave like smooth 1D lines or flat 2D surfaces. Great Britain's coast squiggles so complexly across scales that it has a fractal dimension of roughly 1.25.

Ultimately, a coastline isn't a static geometric object. Its length depends entirely on the tide you choose, the scale of your ruler, and how closely you dare to look.
